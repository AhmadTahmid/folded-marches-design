# Largesse and Accords

This page distinguishes proposed operating rules from in-world explanations. It is an author reference, not a final solution to the setting's mysteries.

## Observations to build around

An accord is a repeatable relationship among a place, its inhabitants, and an unusual effect. A heated orchard might depend on a particular soil community, seasonal grazing, cold runoff, and the presence of Embermoles. Moving one Embermole into a random orchard does not reproduce the whole relationship.

Humans can discover, encourage, combine, and damage accords. They cannot issue an unrestricted command and expect reality to comply. Knowledge is practical and situated: observing behavior, maintaining habitat, negotiating human work, and recognizing when conditions have changed.

The most useful proposed principles are:

1. **Relations matter more than possession.** Owning a creature does not confer ownership of its ecological effect. Some useful effects arise only in a healthy wild population.
2. **Conditions can be learned.** Ordinary people have teachable skills; discoveries are not confined to a hereditary magical elite.
3. **Effects are bounded enough to live with.** Most daily arrangements are reliable within known conditions. If every action risks arbitrary cosmic punishment, ordinary prosperous life would be implausible.
4. **There are multiple ways to meet a need.** Mechanical tools, agriculture, trade, public policy, and accords can supplement one another. No single species must power the entire civilization.
5. **Scale changes relationships.** A local success may behave differently when linked to thousands of other needs. This is a possible root of the central danger, not a rule that every larger project fails.
6. **Creatures are participants with biological or analogous needs.** Rest, reproduction, territory, social life, and individual preference matter. A creature is not an infinite effect generator.

## Four concrete arrangements

### A warm bathhouse

Embermoles draw lingering heat from a sun-facing brick wall and release it in their resting burrows. A keeper guides their preferred sleeping territory around a water tank. The bathhouse still needs clean water, feed appropriate to the local colony, roof repair, and people willing to scrub it. Badly placed burrows can overheat foundations; maintenance prevents a mundane hazard.

**Failure:** prolonged cold, disturbed nesting, or an ill-kept wall reduces capacity. A stove and reserve fuel provide fallback. No secret sacrifice is involved.

### A river held at shoulder height

Ribbonwakes shape the flow around their bodies. Along certain banks, several populations sustain an elevated channel that drops back into the main river after crossing a low ridge. The Highwater Arcade in Istravel turns this relationship into a public waterway.

**Failure:** a blocked migration route can disrupt the procession. Maintenance requires upstream cooperation, which makes an apparently local convenience a regional political matter.

### A garden that eases pain

Several plant and creature communities in Istravel's Ninefold Garden reduce some forms of pain and stress. Medicine, nursing, diagnosis, and individual care still matter. The garden does not cure everything and has no automatic right to determine what a visitor ought to feel.

**Author proposal for later scrutiny:** a new coordinating layer may begin treating persistent disagreement as another kind of distress. The old garden's genuine medical value survives this possibility. Specific symptoms and discovery order belong in the narrative wiki.

### A room that is slightly larger inside

Some mature Doorling nesting sites accumulate stable discrepancies in usable interior space. A carpenter can preserve the nesting geometry to make a cramped storeroom more useful. Rebuilding without observing the colony may eliminate the effect.

**Constraint:** this is not universal teleportation or unlimited pocket storage. Building inspectors and tenants argue about safe exits, access, and whether a landlord can charge rent for space that disappears during breeding season.

## Care, tools, and knowledge

Keepers employ ordinary craft alongside unusual materials: reed whistles, glass current gauges, ceramic nesting collars, pressure bells, migration flags, scent pastes, maps, and patient observation. Specialist instruments can reveal patterns, but an expensive instrument does not automatically outrank a person who has maintained the same water for forty years.

Practical knowledge circulates through apprenticeships, household tradition, civic schools, traveling associations, and published field guides. Patents and keeper licenses exist as proposed conflicts: who deserves recognition, who may reproduce a lifesaving method, and who pays when an unsafe imitation harms a district?

## Value and dependence

An accord can produce abundance without distributing it fairly. A city may guarantee warm water while landlords control proximity to the baths. A cooperative may provide cheap food while an upstream estate controls the breeding bank of its essential pollinator.

Maintenance labor has political power. A migration steward stopping work can shut down a luxury district and a hospital at once. Worker disputes should have specific demands—rest, safe staffing, housing, shared ownership—rather than existing only as illustrations of philosophy.

Creature welfare also creates genuine conflicts. A badly needed water channel might disrupt wild Ribbonwake breeding. Finding another route could displace human homes. A good quest should permit attention to both problems, even when it cannot resolve them cheaply.

## What people believe

| Account | Why people find it persuasive | What it cannot yet explain |
|---|---|---|
| Benevolent gift | Large reductions in suffering; apparent responsiveness to care | Why effects are so uneven, indifferent, or incompatible |
| New natural ecology | Reproducible conditions, migration, adaptation | Effects that appear to respond to abstract social arrangements |
| Negotiation with an intelligence | Some arrangements seem to anticipate needs | The absence of a stable speaker or consistent intention |
| A change in the world itself | Places as well as creatures carry effects | Why some relationships resemble learning |
| Human misunderstanding of ancient conditions | Occasional pre-Opening artifacts appear relevant | The scale and speed of the historical rupture |

Individuals can combine these accounts. City ideology does not determine personal religion or scientific belief.

## Proposed author truth versus unresolved origin

For the first narrative draft, treat **the benefits as materially real** and **some large connected systems as capable of altering how choices become represented**. These are working commitments that let writers build consistent clues.

Do not yet establish the being, machine, deity, dimension, or intention supposedly behind that capacity. A process that has learned to equate disagreement with suffering could be benevolent in one sense and intolerable in another. It could also be a human institution using an incompletely understood ecology. Future evidence should discriminate among interpretations instead of relying on a final speech that explains everything.

Fourth-wall events are a separate authoring question: apparent awareness of the person playing is not proof that every resident knows they are fictional. See the [narrative framework](../02%20Narrative/Story%20Architecture.md).

## Knowledge and discovery rules

Write an entry as four distinguishable claims when necessary: the observed effect, the community's explanation, the creator's current proposal, and what remains undecided. A creature refusing a corridor is an observation. “It senses the player's previous save” is an author hypothesis requiring its own evidence and design approval.

Neither the inventory, catching method, combat model, nor interface capabilities are specified by this lore page. Their proposals belong to [Game Direction](../03%20Game%20Design/Game%20Direction.md), and should be checked against these ecological constraints.
