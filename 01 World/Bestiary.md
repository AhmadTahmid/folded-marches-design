# Bestiary of the Folded Marches

**Visual review, 2026-09-27:** the user rejected the five illustrated 0.3 battle appearances. Their renderings are not species identity masters. [Creature Art Direction and Review](../04%20Production/Creature%20Art%20Direction%20and%20Review.md) keeps expressive and grand appeal open for a small comparison; species ecology below remains draft. Review one appealing original companion before generating the roster in bulk.

**Start with [Creature Roster v0.1](Creature%20Roster%20v0.1.md):** where to meet every creature, which can join, what makes each appealing, and eight developed early/midgame recruitment encounters. This page preserves their fuller biology, appearance and folklore; the roster connects them to play.

The original eighteen proposed species remain below: twelve initial creatures and six travel-oriented additions. The v0.1 pass adds a nineteenth encounter creature, [Latchrunner](Creatures/Latchrunner.md), as a distinct late pursuer. These are draft creatures and encounter designs, not approved art or a finished combat roster. Mount capabilities remain intentions; no final elemental chart, evolution chain, capture rate, or compulsory starter selection is implied.

Species behavior below is **proposed author-side observation**. Folklore is labeled separately. Each individual can vary in temperament. “Partnership” means a plausible long-term relationship; its actual game implementation belongs in the design wiki. A completed field record should be possible for species that cannot be caught.

## Thimbleback

**Appearance and character.** A knee-high burrower with chestnut-brown overlapping shell cups, moss in the seams, small digging hands, and an extravagantly serious nose. Its shell cups collect seeds, rainwater, and whatever the individual considers worth saving. A pleased Thimbleback pats a chosen object before putting it away. A displeased one deliberately puts it somewhere inconvenient.

**Ecology.** Thimblebacks live in the Chestnut Steps and Staired Wood. Their caches spread trees and loosen compacted terrace soil. They eat nuts, roots, and soft invertebrates. Family groups reuse burrows while occasionally exchanging young adults with nearby groups. Foxes and Vergecats prey on them, particularly during travel.

**Human relationship.** Orchard households tolerate small losses in exchange for soil care. A companion may learn gathering routines and locate buried objects by scent; it does not know which objects humans consider valuable. Keeping one healthy requires digging space, varied food, and permission to store things. An excessively tidy home is a poor fit.

**Encounter seed.** A leaking terrace drain contains a Thimbleback's winter store. Redirecting water preserves both the terrace and the cache; simply chasing it out causes the problem to recur elsewhere. Later visits can show the same individual using the improved channel.

**Folklore.** Villagers say a Thimbleback remembers every promise made above its burrow. No evidence establishes supernatural moral judgment. An animal that recognizes who brings food is already observant enough to embarrass people.

**Design opportunity.** An appealing early partner with carrying, scent, and digging associations. Its joy and petty hoarding need no dark reinterpretation.

## Kitefin

**Appearance and character.** An airborne ray with pale translucent fins, a warm-colored body, and two long streamers that curl around useful perches. Circular thin patches in its fins shimmer like soap film. Young individuals follow moving cloth, perform elaborate landing corrections, and sometimes misjudge a washing line with great dignity.

**Ecology.** Kitefins graze on airborne spores, small insects, and moisture-rich particles lifted by warm currents. They nest on the wind saddles and breed near the Cloud Loom. Their eggs adhere beneath sheltered stone lips. Cold rain and disrupted breeding air currents pose greater threats than most predators.

**Human relationship.** Some become attached to familiar traveling groups. People use their behavior as one weather indicator among several. They may carry light objects in fitted harnesses if accustomed to the task. This does not imply player flight or heavy freight: keeping a Kitefin aloft is not the same as lifting a person.

**Encounter seed.** A juvenile repeatedly steals cloth from a fair stall because the wind around its usual resting place has changed. Following it reveals a blocked vent and a tiny roost. The cloth theft can remain comic even when the solution teaches habitat observation.

**Folklore.** “A red tail means tomorrow's rain.” In reality local diet, age, light, and health affect coloration; reliable keepers watch several signs.

**Open question.** The amount of aerodynamic impossibility should remain bounded consistently once movement is designed. Its personality should not depend on being secretly omniscient.

## Bellwether

**Appearance and character.** A compact mountain grazer with broad gripping feet, a soft neck ruff, and two hollow crescent horns that ring faintly when wind crosses their openings. Mineral deposits change each individual's timbre. They stand still with infuriating confidence just where a traveler wants to pass.

**Ecology.** Bellwethers browse scrub on limestone slopes, open paths through tough seasonal growth, and spread seeds in their coats. Their calls and horn resonance help a herd remain connected in fog. Certain long-used paths retain a brief, repeatable acoustic echo that experienced animals follow around terrain humans misread.

**Human relationship.** Highland keepers learn calls rather than issuing universal commands. A bonded individual can assist route finding and difficult ground travel, but may refuse a path based on smell, footing, or remembered disturbance. Refusal is useful evidence, not proof of supernatural danger.

**Encounter seed.** The herd will not approach the road-opening bells. A new decorative vane produces a tone that disrupts the call. Adjusting it restores the gathering without a dramatic revelation. A later refusal can then become meaningful because the player has learned an ordinary explanation first.

**Folklore.** Bells were copied from the horns of an ancestor that led survivors through the Opening. The village story may preserve a practical memory without being literally accurate.

**Design constraint.** Important information carried by sound must also have readable visual or textual expression in the eventual game. This is an accessibility requirement for later design, not a current implementation.

**Individual story proposal:** [Clatter](../02%20Narrative/First%20Companion.md), the optional fair Bellwether, develops a distinct relationship without changing the species' ecology or requiring a particular starter.

## Embermole

**Appearance and character.** A stout, six-clawed digger with coal-dark fur, copper whiskers, and seams of warm color beneath tough skin. It opens fanlike plates along its back to release stored warmth. Youngsters huddle around freshly sunlit bricks; adults compete over the most aesthetically satisfying sleeping hollow.

**Ecology.** Embermoles draw retained heat from suitable material and relocate it through burrows. Their activity changes soil thaw and supports small winter plant communities. They eat roots, fungi, and heat-loving insects; heat alone is not food. Colonies need several temperatures rather than an endlessly hot enclosure.

**Human relationship.** Bathhouses, bakeries, and protected terraces cultivate compatible habitat. Colonies reduce fuel demand but cannot supply limitless heat. Long cold periods, disturbed nests, illness, and damaged source surfaces reduce output. A household companion still needs a safe refuge that is not assigned a productive purpose.

**Encounter seed.** A bakery's output falls because the oldest Embermole has moved its preferred sleeping space beside a new family group. The keeper can restructure the burrows, use a temporary stove, and accept slower production. The creature is not broken machinery.

**Folklore.** An Embermole recognizes a welcoming hearth. Often it recognizes reliable conditions and a keeper who does not disturb it.

**Risk.** Poorly planned burrows can damage foundations or produce burns. This mundane hazard supplies practical stakes without making domestic warmth a concealed curse.

## Glassgrazer

**Appearance and character.** A long-faced, six-legged herd animal with a frosted translucent muzzle and layered mineral fans along its ribs. Its soft body is visible between opaque growths, preventing it from becoming merely a crystal statue. Adults rub their fans on chosen stones and visibly enjoy the resulting scrape.

**Ecology.** Glassgrazers eat wet vegetation and mineral-rich sediment. Their bodies separate certain dissolved materials and shed them as branching deposits. Herd routes form the White Orchard and support lichens, insects, and small pools. Different habitats produce different mineral colors; forcibly feeding decorative minerals is dangerous.

**Human relationship.** Some herds help treat suitable water, while shed material supplies craftwork and specialist glazing. They cannot remove every contaminant. Industrial operators must still measure water quality and preserve grazing, social contact, and migration. A tame herd member can become an expedition partner, though its size and needs complicate travel.

**Encounter seed.** A new treatment channel makes city water cleaner but removes a mineral supply that a downstream wild herd depends on. The solution requires rerouting some flow or restoring habitat; accusing either city workers or wildlife advocates of selfishness misses the material problem.

**Folklore.** A perfectly clear fan proves the animal has never encountered a lie. Actual clarity depends on mineral deposition, wear, and health.

**Open question.** Whether mineral patterns support individualized cosmetic identification. No evolution-by-gemstone system is established.

## Loam Choir

**Appearance and character.** A distributed colony of soft fungal bodies wearing shallow shell caps, with fine rootlike threads between them. When disturbed or exchanging nutrients, the caps vibrate against one another in descending notes. A colony can look like a small village rearranging its rooftops after rain.

**Ecology.** Colonies break down suitable organic matter and build fertile substrate. Their fruiting bodies disperse along damp edges; neighboring colonies may cooperate, compete, or remain distinct. Some are associated with old wood, others with particular agricultural wastes. One cultivation recipe does not suit all populations.

Within a healthy colony, patches can rest or remain quiet while other patches exchange signals. This is observable ecological variation. It may inspire an experiment in a human sharing practice, but it does not establish a moral rule for people.

**Human relationship.** The Choirworks maintain selected colonies for soil and food processing. Safe harvest takes prepared material and natural outputs rather than assuming every visible part is edible. Workers know the difference between healthy variation and distress signals. Overloading a colony can produce contamination and collapse.

**Partnership form.** A small colony could travel in a carefully tended habitat, but should remain a colony with spatial and ecological needs. Presenting one cap as the “real animal” would contradict this concept unless later evidence supports that interpretation.

**Encounter seed.** Workers report that a production target has erased the quiet interval between colony cycles. Output still looks excellent. Restoring the interval requires accepting a short-term shortage or finding another supplier, making welfare and provisioning a concrete negotiation.

**Folklore.** The choir sings the names of the dead beneath the soil. No translation system confirms this. Its sound can be moving without being a hidden message.

**Later mystery boundary.** Shared colony behavior is not evidence that all human dissent should be dissolved into consensus.

## Ribbonwake

**Appearance and character.** A sinuous aquatic creature with a smooth central body, small gripping limbs, and broad transparent fringes that resemble clear silk drawn through water. Colored organs brighten during social display. Resting individuals curl around rocks like ribbons tied loosely around a gift.

**Ecology.** Ribbonwakes shape local flow through coordinated body movement and an associated accord. Populations maintain pools, move sediment, and create sheltered breeding water. They feed on small aquatic organisms and need migration between feeding and spawning sites. Their current-making is bounded by habitat and group organization.

**Human relationship.** Suitable populations support irrigation, crossings, and elevated channels such as Istravel's Highwater Arcade. Keepers protect migration and maintain water quality. No individual is a portable unlimited river. A companion requires access to water and an appropriate travel arrangement; it cannot simply spend an expedition drying in a pocket.

**Encounter seed.** A charming new riverside market occupies the bank needed for spawning. Residents are willing to move stalls, but the alternate site is controlled by a landlord. Solving the ecological problem therefore means dealing with people and space, not defeating a creature.

**Folklore.** Two Ribbonwakes tying their fringes together guarantee a lasting romance. The animals often separate five minutes later to feed.

**Connection.** Reedhouse develops limited sensory-sharing practices around compatible relationships. This does not make all Ribbonwakes willing merger participants or the property of the Confluence.

## Morrow Moth

**Appearance and character.** A hand-to-head-sized nocturnal moth with broad wings whose layered surfaces retain and slowly release some qualities of daylight. At dusk a resting swarm resembles fragments of pale sky pinned to reeds. Their antennae comb moisture from the air in minute, regular movements.

**Ecology.** Morrow Moths pollinate night-blooming wetland plants. The larval stage depends on specific reed beds and completes a seasonal cycle largely beyond settled districts. Artificial light, habitat loss, and collecting adults for ornament threaten local populations.

**Human relationship.** People celebrate their flight and learn weather cues from their roosting positions. The species has no stable individual field-partner behavior in this proposal. A moth may land on a traveler; that is not a lasting bond or authorization to confine it. Its bestiary record rewards observation across stages and protection of habitat.

**Encounter seed.** A festival organizer wants reliable brightness on a night when the moths have moved elsewhere. A beautiful celebration can be redesigned around ordinary lamps and an excursion to observe the undisturbed roost. The player does not need to turn a wild spectacle into a service.

**Folklore.** Its wings show tomorrow's sky. Measured observations indicate responses to current and recent conditions; actual prophecy is not established.

**Author intention.** Preserve at least some encounters with the moths as uncomplicated wonder. A world with horror still needs living things that are simply alive.

## Vergecat

**Appearance and character.** A low, long predator with dark mottled fur, delicate feelers around its cheeks, and pale markings visible only along the moving edge of its body. In broken light its silhouette seems briefly narrower than the animal that emerges from it. Its call sounds like a cough followed by two stones clicking.

**Ecology.** Vergecats hunt hares, Thimblebacks, young grazers, and other manageable prey near interfaces between dense cover and open ground. A localized accord shortens the apparent distance of a prepared pounce. It requires suitable cover, a stable approach, and recovery afterward; it is not arbitrary teleportation.

**Human relationship.** Most avoid settled activity but can endanger livestock and travelers who disturb a den. Removing every Vergecat can increase damaging grazer populations. Coexistence uses protected routes, appropriate stock enclosures, and respect for breeding territory.

**Partnership.** Exceptional individuals may develop trust with specialist keepers; this should require sustained knowledge and should not convert every wild predator into a friendly collectible. No compulsory early capture is proposed.

**Encounter seed.** Repeated livestock losses lead to a request for extermination. Tracks show that a new path cuts through a nursing territory. Alternatives include rerouting, temporary protection, and moving stock. Combat, if later supported, is not the only intelligible response.

**Folklore.** A Vergecat can step into the space between a person's thoughts. This is a story, not established mind-reading.

**Horror boundary.** A frightening animal is still an animal. Its danger should be legible enough that careful observation improves survival.

## Knuckle Saint

**Appearance and character.** A creature the size of a small building moves on immense jointed forelimbs, its compact hindquarters following under a sloping armored back. Moss, flowering plants, and small nesting animals inhabit long-undisturbed surfaces. When resting, it folds into something like a ruined chapel without ever becoming architecture.

**Ecology.** Knuckle Saints follow long seasonal routes between mineral flats and upland feeding grounds. Their passage opens channels, redistributes material, and creates clearings used by smaller species. They eat slowly at widely separated sites and may remain inactive for weeks. No person has documented a complete life cycle in this regional draft.

**Human relationship.** People plan crossings, festivals, closures, and buildings around known routes. Saints cannot be caught or owned. They may recognize familiar sensory signals and alter a step enough to avoid a disturbance, but that is not reliable obedience. Climbing onto one should remain a considered encounter possibility, not a promised mount system.

**Encounter seed.** A new bridge constricts a migration route. Moving the bridge is expensive; redirecting the Saint threatens another habitat. Survey, temporary structures, negotiation, and advance warning can produce several materially different outcomes.

**Folklore.** A Saint kneels wherever a city will one day stand. Old settlements have indeed grown on land made easier to occupy by its rest sites. The ecological explanation may be sufficient.

**Author intention.** Give the player experiences beyond possession. Watching a giant complete its migration should count as a meaningful achievement.

## Velvet Undoer

**Appearance and character.** A pale spiral-bodied decomposer moves on dense ranks of tiny feet beneath a plush fringe. Its upper surface looks invitingly soft. Its feeding edge leaves a neat, oddly polished boundary where decaying material has been removed. Several together can resemble an abandoned rug beginning to roll itself up.

**Ecology.** Undoers specialize in complex dead organic structures and certain bound materials, including aged lacquer, glues, and worked fibers. They inhabit damp ruins, fallen timber, and neglected storage. They do not delete matter or erase concepts: they separate materials through an unusual digestive process whose limits can be learned.

**Human relationship.** They damage equipment, records, buildings, and keepsakes. No reliable domestic application is established; attempts at useful controlled disposal remain hazardous. People protect archives with dry separation, suitable containers, and regular inspection rather than keeping an Undoer as an adorable waste bin.

**Encounter seed.** A refuge store has lost straps and seals just before travel season. Understanding the infestation allows safe isolation and replacement. Killing visible animals alone fails if the damp materials remain suitable habitat.

**Folklore.** Undoers eat memories because objects they damage can carry sentimental meaning. No evidence establishes literal memory consumption.

**Author intention.** A creature can be fascinating without being friendly, useful, uniquely evil, or an allegory. Later mystery scenes must not confuse biological damage with supernatural deletion without evidence.

## Doorling

**Appearance and character.** A small angular scavenger with flexible keratin folds, springy feet, and bright eyes beneath an overlapping brow flap. It can flatten its outline to slip through narrow gaps, then unfold into an unexpectedly round resting shape. Its nest includes sorted scraps of wood, metal, cloth, and paper. Different individuals prefer different categories with inexplicable intensity.

**Ecology.** Doorlings occupy dry edges between old buildings and active human space. Their nests stabilize small spatial discrepancies under specific conditions. A long-used nesting cavity can hold slightly more than its exterior geometry suggests. The effect is local, modest, and dependent on the colony's maintained arrangement.

**Human relationship.** Some household colonies share space with residents; others become frustrating thieves. Craftspeople sometimes preserve nesting geometry in storerooms. A companion can be playful, observant, and possessive about a travel kit. It should not grant arbitrary infinite storage or allow bypassing every locked door.

**Encounter seed.** A landlord claims that tenants have secretly expanded a room. A Doorling colony has moved into the wall after roof repairs. Resolving rent, building safety, and habitat produces a comic civic case with genuine consequences.

**Folklore.** A Doorling knows which doors were never meant to exist. Its actual attraction to seams and crowded thresholds provides ample ordinary explanation.

**Possible late clue.** One individual repeatedly arranges a physical label separately from the object it named. This is behavior the player can observe; any connection to changing records must be earned later. Do not make the entire species secretly aware of game menus.

**Developed quest proposal.** [A Room for the Keeper](../02%20Narrative/Quests/A%20Room%20for%20the%20Keeper.md) develops this behavior in Tavio's familiar individual. The larger confinement effect belongs to a particular coordinating accord, not to an expanded species-wide power. Most Doorling encounters can remain playful or inconvenient without that development.

## Brackenstride

**Appearance and temperament.** A chest-high grazer runs on four slender, spring-jointed legs. Fernlike shoulder fans fold against its russet body, then flare to brake before a jump. Its muzzle ends in a soft fork used to strip seed heads. Social individuals solicit races and become visibly offended when a walking person accepts a race without actually running.

**Ecology.** Brackenstrides browse the sunny scrub above the Chestnut Steps and orchard basin. Hooked seeds travel in their shoulder fans. They shelter their young in dense thorn circles; adults stamp warning patterns before a Vergecat approaches. Burning every scrub patch to improve pasture would remove their nursery habitat.

**Partnership and limits.** Suitable adults carry one rider quickly over open ground and leap broad, visible gaps with adequate landing space. Their advantages are rhythm, distance, and small diversions to interesting overlooks. Loose scree, narrow stairs, dense branches, and blind jumps require dismounting or another approach. Long travel uses ordinary rests, not constant feeding prompts. A practiced runner can mark a passable route through low scrub without flattening a forest.

**Exceptional encounter.** After the first rain following a dry interval, a pale-fanned individual crosses the Bell Road to reach fresh shoots. Split seed husks and fan impressions appear before the crossing; a courier knows where to wait. Following two confirmed signs leads to its sheltered feeding hollow if the crossing is missed. Its pale fans are an individual marking, not a universally superior combat form. Ordinary adults introduce riding sooner.

**Combat counterexample and folklore.** A fast field runner need not deliver high burst damage or survive confinement well. “It outruns bad news” is a courier's joke; the species cannot sense tragedy. Whether its jumps become a combat repositioning technique remains a separate design decision.

## Cragmantle

**Appearance and temperament.** A broad six-footed animal, somewhere between a soft-bodied grazer and a draped stone gargoyle, wears overlapping slate plates above a warm ocher belly. Each foot unfolds into a rosette of gripping pads. It rests by spreading itself over a favorite sunlit boulder and dislikes being hurried off it.

**Ecology.** Cragmantles scrape mineral films and lichens from the Old Switchback's limestone walls. Their feeding opens shallow seep channels used by plants and insects. The pads grip suitable rock through a bounded local accord; dusty, crumbling, or heavily glazed surfaces interrupt it. Eggs develop beneath protected overhangs that adults revisit rather than carry around.

**Partnership and limits.** A familiar adult carries one rider up steep, continuous sound stone. It tests each placement and is slower than climbing stairs. It cannot cling to a ceiling, leap a chasm, or turn any vertical building into a route. It can stabilize a hauling line or help inspect a retaining wall; competent equipment and local climbing guides offer alternatives. Its habit of tapping unsafe stone communicates both through movement and a journal cue.

**Exceptional encounter.** After gentle rain has washed a sheltered gorge wall, old scrape marks become visible beneath a black overhang. A copper-mantled adult feeds there near dusk. Reading fresh pad prints and newly cleared water channels provides a repeatable tracking encounter even if its initial appearance roll fails. The animal's approach lets the player discover a small spring used before the Opening, with mundane wear evidence worth recording.

**Combat counterexample and folklore.** Secure footing is situational protection, not universal armor or immunity to displacement. A Cragmantle can be outmaneuvered on level ground. Masons say it hears a stone's age; the observable behavior is testing grip and vibration. Literal historical perception remains unproposed.

## Sailrook

**Appearance and temperament.** A large bird has four broad wings with pale jointed vanes and black trailing edges. Opening them resembles unfolding a many-sided map. Its small crest swivels toward rising air; its feet are delicate tools for gathering floating seed clusters. It enjoys dropping an inedible object and catching it again, to the annoyance of anyone below.

**Ecology.** Sailrooks nest on isolated shelves around the Cloud Loom. They eat carrion, large insects, and nutrient-rich windborne seed clusters, dispersing some seeds elsewhere. Their mass is partly supported by an accord with established warm air columns, but they still need lift, feeding territory, and safe ledges. Nest defense can make a magnificent animal dangerous.

**Partnership and limits.** A compatible adult carries one rider along rising thermal corridors, banking into extended glides between reachable landing shelves. It does not hover, launch from any street, fly inside caves, or cross a whole region without rest. Rain and cold still air close some routes; a visible forecast and ground path preserve the expedition. Scouting from a legal landing reveals terrain relationships rather than automatically revealing every secret.

**Exceptional encounter.** On a clear afternoon after rain, two thermal plumes meet above the Cloud Loom and shed seeds rise in a turning column. A white-edged Sailrook may descend to investigate. A keeper teaches the seed sign; tracing fallen vanes to a non-nesting rest ledge supplies the authored encounter. No stolen egg or repeated nest raid is required.

**Combat counterexample and folklore.** Excellent gliding does not mean strongest aerial combatant: compact arenas deny its preferred approaches. “It has flown beyond tomorrow” is a mountain story. A long journey or unusual feather coloration establishes no prophecy. The smaller Kitefin retains its light-carrying ecology and cannot substitute as a human mount.

## Vesperveil

**Appearance and temperament.** A large, six-limbed dusk pollinator spreads pleated skin membranes around a narrow, furry body. Rosy veins show through its dark canopy; when folded, it resembles a shawl wrapped around a branch. Fine tasting whiskers brush a person's sleeve before it decides whether to approach. It is patient about stillness and easily overwhelmed by a shouting crowd.

**Ecology.** Vesperveils feed on tall night-blooming flowers near sheltered Cloud Loom gullies and high wetland margins. Some pollen adheres to their face fans. Slow powered flight is assisted by a local accord in cool, humid, relatively still air. Open sun overheats the membranes; strong gusts can injure them. Their roosts are quiet spaces under broad branches and deep stone lips.

**Partnership and limits.** A suitable adult supports one rider in a broad sling during short hovering crossings and vertical approaches among sheltered ledges. It reaches places a gliding Sailrook cannot land, but cannot make fast long-distance trips or cross exposed storm air. During daylight it remains useful for close observation from a quiet resting perch; flight is not permanently available simply because it has been caught.

**Exceptional encounter.** On a calm evening following a humid day, flower cups unfold and release pale pollen. A blue-veined individual visits the highest cluster. The player can learn the same sign from a gardener, observe its shadow, or follow pollen brushed onto an accessible roost. A journal wait at a safe shelter reaches the next suitable evening; no real-world nighttime login is involved.

**Combat counterexample and folklore.** Hovering precision does not imply fast initiative, control immunity, or high damage. Its nectar needs remain ordinary biological needs. “It drinks the words people keep to themselves” describes its quiet manner; it is neither a mind reader nor an automatic horror herald.

## Reedbarque

**Appearance and temperament.** A broad freshwater animal carries a low ridged carapace fringed with flexible reedlike growths. Its blunt head folds under the rim during rest; four oarlike limbs propel it with surprisingly delicate strokes. Small birds visit its back, and some individuals carefully lift one edge to discourage a particularly loud passenger.

**Ecology.** Reedbarques crop underwater vegetation in Low Lantern Fen and the Braided Road waterways. Grazing keeps narrow channels open; eggs and small organisms travel briefly in the wet fringe. Breeding adults need quiet muddy banks. They tolerate brackish margins but not the concentrated Quiet Salt pans, and populations cannot be moved indiscriminately between watersheds.

**Partnership and limits.** A practiced adult provides stable surface travel for a rider and compact expedition supplies. It can hold position at a reed bank or serve as a safe observation platform. It is slow against strong currents, cannot dive with a rider, and cannot climb dry banks or span every flooded obstacle. A local boat can reach the same essential destinations; the creature offers intimacy, quiet access, and useful stopping places.

**Exceptional encounter.** After a rising-water forecast, fresh crescent browsing marks appear above the usual waterline. A many-ringed elder occasionally uses the new channel at dawn. A Reedhouse boatkeeper can help follow its grazing sequence to a known resting pool, with no consumable bait. Inviting an available adult remains distinct from disrupting a nesting parent's duties.

**Combat counterexample and folklore.** Stability is a possible defensive niche, not a promise of exceptional damage. Constrained water access and slow repositioning matter. People call the birds on its back “its council”; the birds are opportunistic passengers, not evidence of a shared humanlike government.

## Gorgecoil

**Appearance and temperament.** A long muscular swimmer has an otterlike head, two grasping forelimbs, and overlapping silver collars over a dark flexible trunk. Its broad tail curls around a rock while it watches passing objects with bright predatory attention. Play involves letting a round stone drift downstream, then retrieving it before the next pool.

**Ecology.** Gorgecoils hunt small fish and large aquatic insects in clear, oxygen-rich gorges below Orthe. An accord with a continuous current lets them keep unusually strong purchase in turbulent water; it does not produce water or pull them through a dry channel. They return to air regularly and use quiet side pools for young. Silting, extreme floodwater, and impassable barriers disrupt their habitat.

**Partnership and limits.** A compatible adult carries one rider in a close harness through readable current lanes and on short submerged traverses between safe surfacing pockets. The rider still needs suitable equipment and a known return path. Air supply and safe destinations are previewed before committing; a failed approach returns to the last pocket rather than trapping the player. Deep-water exploration remains a separate unchosen scope question. Dry arches and engineered lifts provide alternate access to important ruins.

**Exceptional encounter.** When storm runoff has subsided and clear water returns, silver scratches appear on a low current-worn arch. A barred individual plays upstream at first light. Following displaced round stones and fresh collar marks leads to its side pool even if no ambient sighting occurs. Floodwater itself is a closure signal, not the secretly correct recruitment condition.

**Combat counterexample and folklore.** Strong currents support its mobility; dry ground does not. A field traversal niche never guarantees top combat performance. Boaters say it ties rivers into knots. Observed behavior uses existing water and local purchase; it does not control the entire watershed.

## Shared ecology and author checks

Thimblebacks disperse trees whose shade supports some Loam Choir colonies. Glassgrazer deposits build wet pockets used by insects that feed Kitefins and Ribbonwakes at different life stages. Morrow Moths pollinate reed plants important to lowland habitats. Vergecats limit some small grazer populations. Knuckle Saint passage changes where several of these relationships can occur.

These links are a beginning, not a complete food web. Before adding a species, ask what it eats, what needs it, where it reproduces, what limits it, and what it does when humans are absent. Before adding a power, ask what makes it bounded enough for the world around it to remain coherent.

Further biology, visual variants, reproduction details, and creature-to-creature relationships can grow from actual quest needs. Avoid declaring every unusual feature part of one hidden intelligence before the central mystery has been chosen.

The six additions propose breadth in travel rather than six stronger versions of existing animals. Their encounter clues are player evidence; their folk sayings are not author truth. Exact body dimensions, load limits, refuge compatibility, acquisition timing, and combat kits remain open design choices. The user welcomes a broad roster, but no final species count or production capacity is established.
