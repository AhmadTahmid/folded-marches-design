# The Confluence

**Role:** plural communities practicing forms of merging with creatures and accords. It is not a third city-state, one species, a single faction, or a single doctrine. All institutions and terminology below are proposals.

## The invitation

Imagine hearing the pressure of water along your whole house. Imagine spending a winter in the patient warmth of stone without losing the capacity to laugh at a visitor's wet boots. Imagine that the boundary of your body becomes a matter of attention rather than skin, while the people you love still recognize how you interrupt a story.

The Confluence begins with the belief that a human life need not remain confined to its inherited form. For some members, merging is spiritual practice. For others it is medicine, exploration, craft, intimacy, or the most practical way to inhabit a place they love.

Some changed lives are flourishing. Their existence should remain true even if other forms of merging become frightening or coercive.

## What merging can mean

At one end are temporary shared sensations or metabolism: sensing a water channel through a Ribbonwake relationship, sharing warmth with a colony, or participating in a place's rhythm for a few hours. At the other are lasting changes to memory, perception, movement, dependence, and bodily form.

These are different practices with different risks. They should not be represented as interchangeable upgrades on a single progress ladder. A person who retains their usual body is not automatically more independent than someone whose adapted home and relationships support a radically changed body.

Creature participation matters. A human's consent cannot stand in for the needs of the animal or colony involved. Schools disagree about how to recognize refusal in organisms unlike themselves, and some are better at admitting uncertainty than others.

## Three communities

### Reedhouse

At the meeting of the lowland rivers, Reedhouse maintains floating rooms, dry workshops, shallow-water courts, and submerged routes. Its members favor limited practices with regular periods apart. Visitors can try carefully supervised sensory sharing without promising permanent change.

The school supports traveling witnesses, independent care, and records held outside the community. It also has blind spots: founders who experienced easy separation may underestimate how difficult it becomes for someone who has finally found relief from lifelong pain.

The daily atmosphere is curious and domestic. People debate recipes that must suit several metabolisms. Chairs have multiple heights. A musician rehearses against the changing resonance of water beneath the floor. Hospitality includes asking what kind of space lets a visitor rest.

### The Cairn of Many Hands

A highland quarry settlement practices long, often irreversible participation in stone and mineral ecologies. Some residents move slowly; others occupy bodies with additional load-bearing surfaces or senses responsive to pressure. Their public halls emerge through years of shared shaping.

The Cairn takes long-term responsibility seriously. It maintains visitors' housing, outside relationships, and stored testimony about how each member wished to be treated before and during change. Its hardest dispute concerns members who can no longer imagine wanting departure. Is that a fulfilled wish, a transformed preference, or evidence that the possibility of refusal has been damaged? The answer cannot be assumed from appearance.

### The Unfastened

A dispersed mutual-aid association supports people who have left, partly left, or attempted to leave a merging practice. Some remain happily changed. Some need extensive care. Some return to another school. The association defends their accounts without requiring them to condemn everyone who stayed.

It has modest safe houses in both cities and along the Braided Road. Its material needs—housing, medical access, advocates, transport—make the abstract right to exit concrete. Neither city consistently funds it well enough.

## Consent over time

Reputable communities record a proposed change, its known uncertainty, independent witnesses, arrangements for later refusal, and who will provide care if expectations fail. These records are meaningful protections, not a guarantee against every tragedy.

For irreversible changes, *exit* may mean leaving a community, ceasing further integration, gaining independent support, or recovering particular abilities. It cannot honestly promise restoration when restoration is unknown. The wiki should distinguish physical reversibility from political freedom and continuity of care.

No simple test proves that a changed person is “still themselves.” Familiar humor, commitments, memory, relationships, and self-description offer evidence; they can also change in ordinary human life. A good narrative lets the person participate in the discussion rather than making everyone else judge them from outside.

## Public disagreement

Istravel wants knowledge that could improve its systems, but may treat changed people as unusually efficient infrastructure. Orthe offers formal protections, but may withhold recognition while debating whether the applicant fits its legal categories. Confluence schools need resources from both and may compromise to secure them.

Within the movement, some advocate wider access and civic integration; others protect small communities; a minority wants human boundaries to become obsolete. The latter is an actual position people can argue for, not a secret desire shared by every member.

## A recurring person

[Corda-of-Reeds](People%20of%20the%20Marches.md#corda-of-reeds) once worked as a river courier. Their present perception extends through a limited reed-bank accord. They travel with equipment and assistance that support separation from that bank. They can be witty, impatient, affectionate, mistaken, and politically strategic.

Corda wants changed persons to retain legal standing and ordinary relationships. They are also reluctant to discuss one memory whose emotional ownership they no longer recognize. That uncertainty belongs to their experience; it should not cancel everything they say.

## Horror without making difference monstrous

An unfamiliar body, plural vocabulary, or unusual sensory life is not itself evidence of corruption. Horror should attach to coercion, concealed dependence, destroyed alternatives, and the failure to recognize another person's refusal.

**Early observed anomaly:** two members independently describe a difficult choice using a metaphor neither remembers learning. **Benign possibilities:** shared teaching, common sensory experience, repeated conversation. **Later author possibility:** a coordinating process is compressing distinctions between their wishes. This remains part of the wider mystery, not a verdict on merging itself.

The amount of successful separation, enduring comfort, and irreversible loss remains open alongside the user's requested tonal alternatives.
