# Tension, stakes and recovery

**v0.1 reading:** [Adventure Rules v0.1](Adventure%20Rules%20v0.1.md) selects one working interpretation for this edition. The comparisons below remain useful alternatives; they are not all simultaneous requirements. User-selected turn-based combat and the five-active ceiling still govern.

The desired peaks combine three things the user explicitly wants: **a comeback earned through mastery, fear for people or creatures they value, and fast reactions under pressure**. Permanent companion loss is not selected. The user asks to compare lasting consequences with recovery or rescue. Strategic turn-based creature combat remains selected; no adrenaline argument silently replaces it.

Research (working-archive reference) supports a narrower claim than the pasted arguments: interactive games against non-human opposition can produce measurable arousal, but neither identical Dota intensity nor this game's success is established. The proposals below are experience hypotheses.

## What we can and cannot transfer from Dota

The user's reference is the desperate late defense after a long shared effort. Our interpretation is that the attraction includes uncertainty, recognizable danger, a plan that might still work, investment in the outcome, and the release when coordinated execution succeeds. The moment is not simply a large punishment or many buttons.

| Ingredient | Translation to this RPG | Limit or design risk |
|---|---|---|
| A match can swing | An enemy commits to a costly pattern; a prepared response changes the objective | An invisible rescue adjustment would make mastery feel staged |
| Familiar heroes and teammates | Companions have remembered habits; allies have independent aims and commitments | Constant random betrayal encourages treating them as disposable |
| Human opposition | A rival or predator selects among understandable tactics and exploits visible mistakes | Scripted behavior cannot promise human social stakes or endless novelty |
| A threatened Ancient | A crossing, rescue, escape route or person provides a clear defended objective | Campaign destruction is a much harsher consequence than losing one match |
| Fast execution | Optional or story-bounded pursuit tests movement and route knowledge | Continuous dodging during turn menus would constitute a new combat design |
| Relief after victory | A safe rest, reunion and changed relationship let the player feel survival | Endless threat erases contrast and makes return burdensome |

Keep *excitement*, *dread*, *frustration*, *mastery* and *desire to continue* separate. A player saying “I was scared of wasting another hour” is not automatically evidence of a good climax.

## Three compatible layers of intensity

**Tactical pressure:** a turn-based encounter changes after committed actions. The player can inspect and plan, but resources, enemy commitments and objective progress make the next choice consequential. A threat described as “the gate breaks after its next two activations” is urgent without a wall-clock timer. Thinking does not secretly hurt the party.

**Expedition pressure:** the group chooses whether to continue, preserve a remaining tool, take a slower path or withdraw with incomplete success. The player knows the stakes category before committing. Much earlier exploration remains useful if the final attempt fails: route knowledge, friendships and records persist.

**Sensorimotor pressure:** a bounded pursuit or hazardous crossing may test fast movement outside ordinary creature battles. Compare this separately with a turn-based escape variant. No mandatory reaction sequence, hybrid combat system or timer is adopted by this page. [Travel, Mapping and Pursuit](Travel%20Mapping%20and%20Pursuit.md) owns that comparison.

All three can support one expedition, but they should not compete for attention in every moment. Teach each required action in a harmless or low-cost context. A high-stakes scene is a poor place to reveal an undocumented control.

## Stakes that survive a failure

| Outcome model | Lasting consequence | Recovery and future play | Editorial assessment |
|---|---|---|---|
| Immediate retry | Encounter resets from a local checkpoint | Knowledge persists for the player; campaign remains intact | Suitable for practice, first lessons and many action attempts |
| Retreat with partial success | A recovered person survives; a cargo or route opportunity is lost | A later route, invoice or reunion acknowledges the incomplete result | Strong candidate: changes the world without forcing roster replacement |
| Separation and rescue | A companion remains alive but unavailable in a known locality | A finite investigation/rescue has alternate tools or allies; no rare species gate | Strong candidate if absence is short enough and the rescue is an adventure |
| Lasting negotiated change | A person takes different work; a creature acquires a visible scar, preference or changed home | Training/treatment and new scenes support a life after the event | Compare carefully: a scar should not mean a permanently useless creature |
| Irrecoverable local loss | An object, habitat feature or time-bound opportunity cannot be restored | Alternatives keep the journey viable; grief receives an aftermath | Suitable only where the loss is comprehensible and not merely a grind tax |
| Permanent companion death | A beloved individual cannot return | A fundamentally harsher campaign; attachment and collection goals change | Preserved as an unselected spark, not the baseline requested by the pop-up |
| Destruction of saves or hours of unrelated collection | Player cannot recover actual accumulated work | Undermines the continuity used to form attachment | Recommend against; no such action or implementation is authorized |

Recovery should not be an unlimited instant undo of every consequence. Saving a separated partner can still leave the rescue site changed, a competing opportunity missed or a relationship altered. Conversely, “permanent” should not be decorative marketing followed by a hidden effortless resurrection.

Injury, strain and rescue must not create cumulative death spirals. A player who lost a key partner gets a viable borrowed team, alternate method or appropriately scaled task. New companions remain usable without rebuilding months of training. A failed objective should often shorten the next attempt instead of making the same preparatory hour compulsory again.

## Paper encounter: hold the crossing, not the whole campaign

**Working scene, not a canonical quest:** a group is carrying stranded travelers across a folded maintenance bridge. The ordinary route was explored earlier; the protagonist has practiced pulling its handline. A predator reaches the near landing, and an ally needs three **completed rounds** to release the far-side brace and finish the crossing. Turn menus pause the decision. The clock counts committed game rounds, not seconds spent reading.

**Objective:** keep the crossing's brace intact until evacuation completes, then withdraw. Driving off the predator sooner also succeeds. A complete kill is unnecessary. There are at most five friendly active creatures; fewer can participate in a separately authored version. Human allies perform the stated objective progress, not extra hidden combat turns.

**Visible danger:** one enemy anchors the main attack, another threatens the rear. The first announces a heavy front-line strike, then a reach attack on the brace. The intentions specify whether they follow a creature or a position and show their fallback rules. The brace is a disclosed encounter object, not a free sixth friendly actor. Its durability and the real damage values must be authored before numerical balancing; this page does not override existing creature kits.

**First exchange:** an inexperienced player spends every resource on damage. The frontline holds, but the brace loses durability. A better sequence saves a protection, interrupts the strongest announced hit and uses the remaining attacker to reduce the flank's next threat. Both routes continue; a mistake does not automatically fail the encounter.

**Middle reversal:** the predator changes its visible target from the defended partner to the brace. The player has one full friendly decision before that activation. A reserve guard, a legal row move, a timed interruption or earlier damage can each matter. Counterplay comes from resources withheld and enemy commitments understood, not from acquiring the single prescribed creature.

**Clutch finish:** one partner is exhausted and one interruption is unavailable. Rather than demand an unexplained resurrection, the remaining team buys one round through a displayed barrier or accepted partial retreat. A human ally can report that the final traveler is across. The player chooses between leaving cargo and spending the last available setup on a better result.

**Failure branch:** the brace collapses into its lower catch platform, already visible on approach. The group survives with an incomplete rescue or a separated companion, according to the selected stake model. The missing individual has a location and evidence; the player does not simply receive “creature deleted.” A nearby rest and alternate recovery route prevent an immediate resource spiral. An automatic rewind variant remains available for comparison.

**Aftermath:** a rescued traveler remembers who was carried first. The companion that held the line wants room to lie down and refuses another crossing that evening. At rest, someone says, “You got us over. Sit down.” Give the player an ordinary minute before another ominous clue. This is where victory becomes felt consequence.

The tactical version should be written and checked before adding a chase. An optional real-time evacuation can reuse the learned route, but its controls, checkpoint and recovery presentation require an independent comparison. It cannot secretly move the bridge's round clock while a menu is open.

## Intelligent opposition that plays a game with the player

Separate **opponent knowledge** from **author knowledge**. A recurring rival can know they observed a repeated opening. A predator can hear a noisy ability or follow a marked trail. Neither reads an uncommitted menu selection unless the chosen story explicitly establishes that perception—and even then the player needs another usable channel.

An adaptive encounter may choose among a small repertoire before commitment: attack an exposed flank, protect against a repeated resonance setup, or pressure a visible objective. Show the sign and retain at least two workable answers. Do not grant an invisible immunity the moment the player chooses a successful move. Saving, pausing or changing accessibility settings are not tactics the opponent punishes.

Replay value can come from terrain, objectives, team composition and a lawful repertoire. It does not require invasions, ranks, asynchronous multiplayer or an omniscient learning model. Those are separate unselected game concepts, not unavoidable substitutes for humans.

## What to compare on paper now

Write the same objective with immediate retry, partial retreat and separation/rescue outcomes. Ask what is lost, what remains worthwhile, what the player can understand before entry and whether a second attempt differs. Then compare a turn-based escape with the short pursuit version. Do not claim equal visceral intensity from prose alone.

Later play should ask: what was at risk; what did the player deliberately change; did a mistake leave a meaningful choice; did the opponent feel fair; did relief arrive; did the player want another expedition? A complete route and passing tests do not answer these questions.
