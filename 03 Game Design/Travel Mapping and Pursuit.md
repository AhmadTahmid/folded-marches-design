# Travel, mapping and pursuit

**v0.1 reading:** [Adventure Rules v0.1](Adventure%20Rules%20v0.1.md) selects one working interpretation for this edition. The comparisons below remain useful alternatives; they are not all simultaneous requirements. User-selected turn-based combat and the five-active ceiling still govern.

The user wants speed to matter, more interaction with the environment, fewer filler battles and a map that becomes available through exploration. A later fast predator or eldritch pursuit is a candidate. This page develops travel rules and compares escape formats; it does not select real-time creature combat or commission implementation.

## Maplessness versus a discovered map

The user's “No map…only unlocks after exploration (concurrent)” is provisionally interpreted as **no prefilled omniscient map; travelled routes are recorded as they are explored**. This preserves concurrent discovery rather than requiring a cartography chore before basic orientation. Literal no-map play and hand-drawn surveying remain alternatives, not secretly adopted requirements.

| Map layer | Proposed contents | What it should not reveal |
|---|---|---|
| Heard account | A destination name, a rough direction and an attributed sketch | Exact unvisited terrain, spawn timers or a reliable route the speaker has never taken |
| Travelled route | Recognizable junctions and connections recorded during ordinary movement | Automatic knowledge of every interior and secret behind a wall |
| Surveyed place | Useful shape and confirmed landmarks after actual investigation | A perfectly accurate universal minimap despite an established local spatial anomaly |
| Personal marks | Shelter, return route, animal signs and unanswered observations | A requirement to place dozens of pins to remember the basic quest |
| Anomaly record | Original route next to a later conflicting account | Silent erasure of the first map or contradictory terrain disguised as user error |

Support orientation through terrain, signs, sun where relevant, inhabitants and concise route notes. Core accessible navigation can highlight a discovered next landmark or recap the last junction. It need not spoil unexplored secrets. Avoid demanding excellent visual memory or audio localization to proceed.

Maps can remain beautiful imperfect objects. A journal history preserves what the player confirmed even if a diegetic page is later altered. Discovering a shortcut changes future travel; repeatedly opening the same menu does not.

## Speed should change a choice

Slower is not automatically more immersive. A deliberate walk is worthwhile when it exposes a behavior, conversation or landmark; running is worthwhile when the route is familiar or time matters. Compare walking, sustained running, a companion-assisted climb, mounted travel and guided transport without selecting exact speeds yet.

- Ordinary ground permits a comfortable traversal pace. Do not add frequent empty stamina waits to lengthen a small map.
- Rough, icy or wet terrain changes approach through clear surfaces and preparation. Surface penalties are visible before a dangerous commitment.
- A bulky load or cautious creature can slow a specific journey, with a short alternative task or paid helper. Do not burden every outing with escort-speed walking.
- Faster companions reveal useful shortcuts and characteristic movement. The main route offers tools, guides or another method when the specialist is absent.
- Repeated safe travel becomes shorter through known routes or transport. An important first crossing can retain its drama.

Movement speed in the world is not an extra combat activation statistic. A fast predator can move quickly between decision points while still obeying the selected battle turn structure.

## Environment interaction as adventure

Every important obstacle should support an intelligible action and payoff: test a handline before using it, listen to a marked crossing, watch where prey refuses to feed, compare meltwater on two ledges or shelter a signal from wind. Some tasks use a companion's ability, some use equipment and some use observation.

Do not put a unique species lock on every branch. A longer ordinary route, a local helper or a loaned tool can reach essential destinations. Specialist collection changes ease, discoveries and style. Unnecessary interaction prompts on every plant would make environmental attention another chore.

A field action normally uses a contextual choice after preview. Repeating the same unlocked action along a familiar route can be streamlined. Keep one memorable obstacle over twelve identical button presses.

## Fewer battles, more reasons for each

A battle earns its place by teaching a tactical relation, presenting a rival, defending something, supporting recruitment, changing a route or offering a voluntary satisfying rematch. Visible wildlife is not an automatic combat trigger. Observation, avoidance and ordinary play all enrich a populated route.

No chosen encounter count or XP rebalance follows here. Growth remains available through exploration and consequential encounters; if repeated filler fights are removed, later numerical progression must be adjusted accordingly. Optional keeper grounds can support players who enjoy battle without forcing everyone to farm them.

## Candidate pursuer: the long hunger

The user's Lifestealer prompt suggests a fast, hungry pursuer whose movement contrasts with the young man. **Working title only:** the long hunger. This is an encounter/appearance brief, not an added bestiary species or an imported Dota character.

**Visual possibility:** a low, long animal with broad gripping forelimbs, a narrow springing rear and a jaw protected by mobile soft folds rather than ornamental spikes. Its frightening feature is how rapidly a seemingly tired body closes distance. Give it one identifying movement—stillness followed by a short, grounded burst. Final anatomy, limb count, ecology and sprite style need a separate creature brief before production.

Compare an ordinary apex predator defending a feeding corridor with a local eldritch organism pursuing repeated attention or a specific carried accord. Their triggers differ; do not combine both explanations by default. It should have boundaries and competing needs, not simply teleport behind the player wherever convenient.

**Foreshadowing:** clawed supports above a path, scattered prey routes, a guide's account of where it will not cross, then a safe distant view of its burst. The player learns one countermeasure on a smaller threat or a harmless practice crossing. A late pursuit pays off knowledge rather than announcing a new sprint button.

## Compare two escape formats

| Format | Pressure | Player choices | Limits |
|---|---|---|---|
| Turn-based escape | The pursuer advances at disclosed encounter stages; hazards and objectives alter the next exchange | Protect a partner, spend a creature activation on an anchor, choose cover or withdraw | No hidden real-time clock; current battle rules need stated encounter interactions |
| Bounded real-time chase | A short route tests movement and previously learned timing | Run, use cover, trigger one practiced helper action, choose an alternate branch | Separate controls and forgiving checkpoints; no simultaneous command-menu dodging or mandatory hybrid combat adoption |

Both can portray the same creature and reveal the same story evidence. Do not claim they feel equivalent before later testing. A reduced-reaction option can stage movement at decision points while retaining consequences and story. Choosing it must not lock a lesser ending.

## Real-time scene study, if that option is later chosen

The party enters a marked forest boundary to recover an agreed objective. The route has three known features: a narrow root arch, a broken service gate and a river shelf. The predator's burst is faster than the protagonist, but it has to turn and cannot pass the root arch at full speed. Straight-line running is therefore insufficient; knowledge and cover matter.

First, an early burst chases the player toward the arch with generous spacing. A companion's practiced signal opens the gate, or a carried tool does so in the alternate route. Near the shelf, the player chooses between abandoning cargo for the broad exit and taking a tighter route to keep it. The choice is made before the final burst; controls do not invert during it.

A missed movement reaches a lower safe catch or local checkpoint. An authored separation outcome can place one companion in a known recovery area, under the selected recovery comparison. No random deletion of hours of collection, punishment for alt-tab or continuing attacks while the game is paused. The pursuit ends at a visible ecological boundary or completed objective; another sudden threat does not immediately erase relief.

Duration remains unmeasured. The intended pressure segment is short compared with the full expedition; a forty-five-minute chase would likely crowd out deliberation and ordinary discovery. Write the whole escape and re-entry on paper before choosing it.

## How a monster can catch the player

Defeat can lead to a trapped camp, a lost carried objective, separated partners or a recovery task. The player retains a usable route back into play. Captivity needs actual decisions—observe a routine, signal an ally, exploit a boundary—not an unskippable humiliating scene followed by item subtraction.

Ordinary wilderness mistakes do not automatically trigger a lasting penalty. Optional marked depths or late story incursions can raise the stakes with comprehensible warnings. A safe village return remains possible in the broader game; no secret global chase begins merely because the player enjoyed side content.
