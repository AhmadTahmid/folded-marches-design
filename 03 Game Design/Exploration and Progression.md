# Exploration and progression

## An open world made of connected lives

The region should feel open because the player chooses routes, purposes, loyalties, and the order of discoveries. Continuous loading-free terrain is a separate technical choice. Mountain passes, river journeys, terraces, city districts, migration paths, and exceptional spaces can form an interconnected world even if they are built as separate regions.

The [Atlas](../01%20World/Atlas.md) owns geography. This page owns the rules for moving through it.

## Four kinds of access

| Access | Example | Avoid |
|---|---|---|
| Physical knowledge | Recognize a seasonal traverse or read a creature's safe path | A route that works only after a quest flag despite visible evidence |
| Companion capability | Cross a gust, stabilize a floating landing, communicate with a herd | One mandatory species blocking the main story |
| Social permission | Travel with couriers, join a pilgrimage, gain sanctuary access | Every faction gate becoming a generic reputation threshold |
| Preparation | Shelter, food, a guide, protective equipment | Routine inventory chores crowding out exploration |

Important routes should generally offer more than one access method. A specialist companion can make a journey safer, shorter, or richer without making every other team invalid.

## Progression rewards

The user wants leveling and satisfying RPG growth, with much less repetitive grinding. Numerical progression is therefore part of the direction; its actual curve and combat rules remain design proposals. Discovery, consequential encounters, and varied objectives should help a traveling team grow without obliging the player to farm a single patch of wildlife. Combat details belong in [Encounter Design](Encounter%20Design.md).

Exploration also rewards new relationships, field techniques, useful knowledge, routes, camp services, expanded refuge capacity, and better interpretation of earlier evidence. A shortcut can change the meaning of a journey. A migration record can settle an argument at a bridge. A companion's preferred resting place can reveal a person the player would otherwise miss. Rewards need not all be containers of currency.

[Rare Encounters](Rare%20Encounters.md) proposes weighted surprises and reliable investigations under learnable conditions. [Mounts and Field Abilities](Mounts%20and%20Field%20Abilities.md) proposes multiple land, air, and water partners plus other uses. Neither system requires a mandatory species or a field specialist occupying one of the five combat places.

## Discovery order

Regional quests can introduce several independent clues. A central revelation requires enough understanding to interpret evidence, rather than all clues in a fixed sequence. If the player reaches a later place early, it still has ordinary inhabitants, activities, and mysteries. Its landscape should not remain empty waiting for the plot.

Narrative knowledge and world changes are separate. Hearing a rumor does not automatically enact the event described. Conversely, a city can visibly change before the player understands why.

## Rest, return, and familiarity

Resting places have hosts, recurring small rituals, and views worth revisiting. Fast travel, if used, should preserve these relationships through route stations or companion-assisted journeys. Routine backtracking can be shortened while major first crossings remain experiences.

The mountain village should remain a home capable of changing, disagreeing, and carrying on without the protagonist. It is not merely a tutorial abandoned after an hour.

## A readable expedition

Before leaving, the player can learn likely weather, route uncertainty, and major travel demands. The journal suggests preparations without publishing every surprise. Unexpected events can alter opportunities; they should not routinely invalidate a carefully prepared team.

### Worked journey: the spring above the road

The protagonist hears that drinking water once reached an abandoned rest house above the Old Switchback. A mason draws a six-petaled pad mark and mentions that the upper rock face becomes readable after rain. The journal records a reported animal, a confirmed place, and a next-day clearing. The player's combat team can remain their preferred five.

At the gorge, washed mineral streaks and fresh lichen scrapes lead upward. The player can walk a longer stair route with a local guide, ascend suitable stone with a Cragmantle, or approach a sheltered intermediate ledge using a Vesperveil during a suitable evening. The last approach still leaves a short climb; no mount solves the whole site. Tools are borrowed locally if needed; crafting materials are not a gate.

At the shelf, the discovery is a spring, an old public drinking trough, and a copper-mantled Cragmantle feeding nearby. A lucky ambient sighting may have revealed it earlier. Following the confirmed signs reaches it without repeated spawn rolls. Observing rather than recruiting completes the ecological discovery. An invitation becomes possible through a short, clearly signaled interaction if this individual is available for partnership.

The trough's worn grooves show that the spring once served travelers from both cities. Recording that evidence supports an optional later restoration conversation. The animal's path also reveals a safe descent that becomes a convenient return route. These are material rewards: a meeting, local history, practical navigation, and a new relationship if desired. No cosmic revelation is required.

The site's weather, character, and restoration details are new proposals. The [Atlas](../01%20World/Atlas.md) continues to own the Old Switchback's established draft geography.

## Exploration friction worth keeping

[Travel, Mapping and Pursuit](Travel%20Mapping%20and%20Pursuit.md) develops the user's new explored-map and meaningful-speed requests: progressively recorded routes, environmental actions, fewer filler fights and contrasting escape formats. This is a provisional interpretation of concurrent map discovery, not selected literal maplessness. [Atmospheric Frontiers](../01%20World/Atmospheric%20Frontiers.md) briefs mist, layered forest, snow and a faded trading settlement without fixing their geography or replacing the existing Atlas.

Keep reading terrain, choosing an approach, judging a visible hazard, making an interesting detour, and deciding when to return. Shorten repeated known journeys after their first meaningful crossing. Avoid empty stamina waits, compulsory gathering stops, repeatedly opening menus to cross consecutive obstacles, and silent random weather failures.

An optional difficult route can challenge knowledge and preparation without punishing ordinary experimentation. Failure should normally return the player to a known safe point with clearer information. Collecting every mount is an expressive goal, not required homework before the next region.

## Long-range possibilities

An accord might change how two places connect, making a familiar path arrive beside a different river. Such changes need stable anchors, clear visual language, and a record of what the player has observed. Keep a distinction between an intentional impossible route and bad navigation. The feasibility of changing geography remains untested.
